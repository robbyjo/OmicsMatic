# Introduction

Understanding how adiposity is reflected in molecular activity could help distinguish systemic responses to excess body mass from processes involved in its metabolic consequences. Blood transcriptomic studies have already shown that body mass index (BMI) is associated with broad differences in gene expression across human populations [32]. These observations establish blood RNA as a useful readout of adiposity-related biology, but leave a question about the resolution of that readout: does the distribution of transcripts produced by a gene carry reproducible information beyond the gene's overall abundance?

A gene can produce multiple RNA transcripts through alternative splicing and differences in transcription initiation or termination [1,2]. Total gene expression summarizes their combined abundance, whereas an isoform ratio measures the fraction assigned to one transcript. A change in that fraction can therefore be obscured when transcripts are collapsed to a single gene-level quantity. Human adipocyte differentiation experiments have found dynamic splicing changes that differ with metabolic phenotype and overlap only partly with changes in gene expression [34]. Genetic studies likewise connect RNA processing with complex-trait variation [3]. These findings motivate a transcript-level analysis of adiposity, while leaving the mechanism underlying any individual ratio association to be established.

Whole blood offers an accessible compartment in which to examine these relationships across independently sampled populations. Its RNA signal integrates multiple blood-cell populations, making it relevant to systemic physiology while also sensitive to cellular composition. Comparisons within the Framingham Heart Study have shown that whole blood and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) differ substantially in their expression profiles [33]. Reproducibility within a common sample compartment and transferability across compartments are therefore separate questions. A whole-blood design permits the former to be examined without treating PBMC measurements as equivalent.

The closest evidence addresses complementary parts of the problem. Population blood studies establish gene-level BMI associations [32]; adipocyte experiments demonstrate metabolic variation in splicing [34]; and isoform-ratio quantitative trait locus studies establish inherited regulation of relative transcript abundance [19]. What remains to be determined in the present cohorts is which adiposity-associated transcript ratios reproduce after adjustment for total gene expression, how stable their estimated effects are across populations, and which warrant genetic and functional follow-up. Resolving these questions would turn a broad catalog of associations into a more specific set of transcript-level hypotheses.

We examined BMI and obesity associations in three TOPMed cohorts with whole-blood RNA, using the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) for discovery and the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults study (CARDIA) and Women's Health Initiative (WHI) for replication. We combined cohort estimates, assessed heterogeneity, and examined functional annotations. We then evaluated all significant meta-analysis transcripts for cis-isoform-ratio genetic instruments and Mendelian randomization (MR). Jackson Heart Study (JHS) PBMC results provided a supplementary assessment of transferability. The primary aim was reproducible association; genetic follow-up was exploratory prioritization.

# Methods

## Study populations and molecular phenotypes

FHS is a multigenerational community-based cohort [7,8]. CARDIA recruited Black and White women and men from four US centers [9], and WHI recruited postmenopausal women [12]. These three cohorts supplied whole-blood association results. JHS, a community-based study of African-American adults, supplied PBMC results [10]. The whole-blood restriction was adopted after inspection of an earlier four-cohort analysis and was not a prespecified tissue comparison. Available participant characteristics are reported in Table 1 as provisional phenotype-file summaries. Exact analysis membership, cohort visits, exclusions, and study-specific ethics and consent statements remain author-completion items.

We analyzed supplied gene-expression-adjusted association summaries without refitting participant-level models. The investigator-described protocol treated BMI as the response and isoform ratio as a predictor, adjusting for age, sex where applicable, white blood cell proportions, batch, and pedigree in FHS; the updated summaries also adjusted for total gene expression. The protocol described TMM-normalized abundance [13] and an isoform ratio equal to a transcript's abundance divided by the summed abundance of its gene's transcripts. Downstream annotation used versioned GENCODE v48 transcript identifiers on GRCh38. Exact cohort-specific formulas, transformations, relatedness specification, and upstream quantification annotation still require confirmation.

The inherited protocol defined obesity as BMI >=30 kg/m2 with a normal-weight comparator below 25 kg/m2. The supplied summaries do not resolve current case/control coding, handling of BMI from 25 to below 30, or the binary-outcome model. Separately, the available descriptive participant table used BMI >30 kg/m2; that strict threshold is retained and explicitly labeled. Neither this table nor feature-specific sample sizes establishes the population or phenotype coding used in each association test. Coefficients retain their supplied model scales.

## Quality control, discovery, and replication

Source records required unique transcript identifiers and finite coefficients, standard errors, test statistics, R-squared, P values, and sample sizes for both phenotypes; standard errors and sample sizes had to be positive, and P values were restricted to [0,1]. A P-value-blind screen then flagged absolute robust coefficient z scores >100 separately within each cohort and phenotype. The location was the median and the scale was 1.482602218505602 times the median absolute deviation, with interquartile range divided by 1.348979500392164 as a fallback; screening required at least 20 features. Primary eligibility was the three-cohort intersection after excluding the union of FHS, CARDIA, and WHI flags. JHS did not determine this universe. This heuristic addresses extreme coefficients; an unfiltered three-cohort sensitivity analysis was retained.

Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rates (FDRs) were calculated separately for BMI and obesity over their complete primary transcript universes [14]. FHS discoveries had FDR <0.05. Within that discovery subset, CARDIA and WHI P values were each adjusted again by BH; reduced FDR <0.05 defined statistical replication. Direction-concordant replication was tabulated separately. Pearson coefficient correlations were calculated over the complete universe and, separately, among transcripts significant in both cohorts being compared. The selected and unselected correlations describe different transcript populations.

## Meta-analysis and functional annotation

Primary pooling used fixed-effect inverse-variance weights, a coefficient of sum(w beta)/sum(w), standard error sqrt(1/sum(w)), and normal-theory two-sided tests and 95% confidence intervals [15]. Multiplicity correction covered every tested transcript within each phenotype. Cochran Q and I2 described heterogeneity [16]. DerSimonian-Laird random-effects estimates with normal-theory intervals were a sensitivity analysis on the identical universe [31]. Pooling assumes compatible model scales; with three cohorts and unresolved upstream transformations, both pooled magnitudes and heterogeneity require cautious interpretation.

Genes mapped from significant meta-analysis transcripts were tested for overrepresentation in Gene Ontology (GO) and Reactome [17,18]. Genes were deduplicated, and the background included all genes mapped from tested transcripts, including those without collection membership. Upper-tail hypergeometric tests included terms with 10-500 background genes and retained zero-overlap terms. BH correction was separate for each phenotype and collection. Resource releases and full term-level results are specified in the Supplementary Methods. These are gene-level annotations of transcript-selected sets.

## Genetic follow-up

Every significant meta-analysis transcript entered cis-irQTL eligibility review using exact versioned transcript identifiers and European-ancestry FHS summaries, in the context of the published isoform-ratio resource [19]. The selected July-August 2026 exports required at least 200 observed samples, at least 40 ratios strictly between zero and one, and Hardy-Weinberg P >=1e-10. The source exports were filtered at P <=1e-7 rather than providing complete regional statistics. Standard errors were reconstructed as abs(coefficient/T), using the supplied definition T=coefficient/SE. A corrected derived input was used where coefficient and R-squared field labels had been interchanged; the correction and source restrictions are detailed in Supplementary Methods.

Outcomes were independent UK Biobank-only Pan-UKB European-ancestry summaries: inverse-rank-normal BMI in 419,163 participants and ICD-10 E66 obesity in 15,917 cases and 404,614 controls [23]. Exposure positions were projected from GRCh38 to GRCh37 with a local chain and matched using strand-aware alleles. Eligible instruments were biallelic cis SNPs with rsIDs, P <=5e-8, and F >=10. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning used 503 unrelated European participants from the high-coverage 1000 Genomes resource [24], a call-rate threshold of 0.95, minor allele frequency >=0.01, and retained-pair r2 <=0.001. All eligible variants were considered per transcript.

A single retained instrument yielded a Wald ratio; two or more yielded inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) estimates [25]. The primary result was fixed-effect IVW when available and otherwise the Wald ratio. Multiplicative-random-effects IVW was available for multi-instrument tests; MR-Egger, weighted median, and leave-one-out analyses were attempted with at least three instruments [21,26]. Weighted-median bootstrapping used 1,000 replicates and seed 2026. BH correction included all estimable primary tests within each outcome, with an additional sensitivity family assigning P=1 to unestimated selected transcripts. FHS observational and irQTL samples overlap; independent outcome data therefore do not remove selection bias or winner's curse [20]. BMI MR estimates are on the transformed outcome scale and obesity estimates are log odds for ICD-coded obesity, rather than the observational BMI-threshold phenotype.

## Supplementary and planned analyses

JHS comparisons were limited to primary transcripts also eligible in JHS. Three- and four-cohort meta-analyses used the same matched set, and JHS replication used the available FHS discovery subset. Earlier partial-region colocalization results were not carried forward as evidence for the current transcript selections. Network and mediation analyses remain planned; neither contributes a result to this manuscript.

# Results

## Study population and analytical scope

FHS contributed discovery, and CARDIA and WHI contributed independent replication within the whole-blood analysis (Figure 1). Table 1 presents the available phenotype-file characteristics; verified association-population totals are unavailable. Per-transcript BMI sample sizes ranged from 18 to 5,027 in FHS, 14 to 3,079 in CARDIA, and 10 to 2,348 in WHI. After validity and coefficient screening, the primary universes comprised 224,365 transcripts for BMI and 223,927 for obesity. These are transcript counts, not participant counts.

## Most FHS discoveries replicated in at least one whole-blood cohort

FHS identified 39,262 BMI-associated transcripts and 25,531 obesity-associated transcripts. CARDIA statistically replicated 27,686 BMI discoveries, including 26,944 with concordant direction; WHI replicated 19,490, including 19,391 with concordant direction. For obesity, CARDIA replicated 18,919 discoveries (18,571 concordant), and WHI replicated 11,139 (11,121 concordant). At least one validator replicated 30,747 BMI and 20,283 obesity discoveries, while both validators replicated 16,429 and 9,775, respectively (Table 2).

Agreement was stronger among selected associations than across all tested transcripts. Pairwise coefficient correlations over the complete universe ranged from 0.233 to 0.363 for BMI and 0.186 to 0.306 for obesity (Figure 3). Among transcripts significant in both FHS and WHI, the corresponding correlations were 0.882 and 0.879. The latter estimates characterize a selected subset and do not represent genome-wide agreement.

## Meta-analysis identified widespread associations with substantial heterogeneity

Fixed-effect meta-analysis identified 61,299 BMI and 48,973 obesity transcript associations at BH FDR <0.05. PCBP2 transcript ENST00000553064.6 had the smallest pooled P value for both traits: 8.84e-161 for BMI and 2.31e-99 for obesity. For BMI, its pooled coefficient was 182.11 (95% CI 168.90 to 195.32) on the supplied scale, with I2=97.7%. Selected estimates appear in Table 3, and cohort-specific estimates for the leading transcripts are shown in Figure 2.

Heterogeneity was widespread rather than confined to PCBP2. Median I2 among significant transcripts was 66.6% for BMI and 59.4% for obesity. On the same test universes, random-effects sensitivity analysis identified 29,150 and 22,981 significant transcripts, respectively. Thus, many associations remained detectable when between-cohort variation was incorporated, while the extent of discovery depended materially on the pooling model.

## RNA-related functional annotations accompanied the association signals

The BMI transcript-selected gene set was enriched for 3,472 of 7,090 tested GO terms and 1,085 of 1,650 Reactome pathways. The obesity set was enriched for 3,736 GO terms and 1,115 Reactome pathways. Leading annotations included RNA processing, splicing, and related cellular functions (Figure 4). Shared genes link many of these terms; their counts should not be read as numbers of independent mechanisms. Disease-named pathways, including a SARS-CoV infection annotation, indicate overlapping gene membership rather than infection in study participants.

## Genetic follow-up prioritized a subset of the observed associations

Among 61,299 BMI-selected transcripts, 21,645 had estimable primary MR results: 17,903 used one instrument and 3,742 used multiple instruments. There were 3,877 FDR-significant estimates, including 3,189 single-instrument estimates. Among 48,973 obesity-selected transcripts, 18,235 were estimable: 14,999 single-instrument and 3,236 multi-instrument tests. Of 150 FDR-significant estimates, 121 used one instrument. Including unestimated selected transcripts as P=1 in the multiplicity family reduced the significant counts to 2,766 for BMI and 105 for obesity.

The smallest BMI MR P value was observed for RBM5 transcript ENST00000496179.5, with a Wald estimate of -21.86 (95% CI -24.68 to -19.04; P=3.61e-52). For obesity, WWP2 transcript ENST00000568818.1 had a two-instrument IVW estimate of 20.91 (95% CI 14.66 to 27.16; P=5.51e-11). Table 4 also includes RBM6, SNRPC, NCF1C, and CLN3 transcripts selected under the stated display rule. The large coefficients retain the molecular-exposure and outcome scales used in MR; they are not clinical effects per typical within-person change in transcript ratio.

Instrument availability constrained follow-up. Before outcome matching, allele harmonization, and LD pruning, strong candidates were available for 745 of 1,502 selected transcripts on chromosome 20, 399 of 695 on chromosome 21, 908 of 1,679 on chromosome 22, and 235 of 2,002 on chromosome X. These denominators combine the BMI and obesity selected transcripts. Unestimated transcripts remain unresolved rather than genetically negative. Available secondary estimators and complete status accounting are retained in the supplementary result tables.

## Transfer to PBMCs was limited

In JHS, one of 39,102 available BMI discoveries met reduced-FDR replication with concordant direction, and none of 25,450 obesity discoveries replicated. On matched transcript sets, adding JHS changed the number of fixed-effect associations from 61,162 to 55,351 for BMI and from 48,877 to 40,022 for obesity. These results limit generalization beyond the whole-blood cohorts. Because cohort and sample compartment vary together, they cannot isolate a PBMC-specific effect.

# Discussion

Relative transcript abundance was broadly associated with BMI and obesity across three whole-blood cohorts, and most FHS discoveries reproduced statistically in at least one independent cohort. The main contribution is a set of transcript-level associations that persists after adjustment for total gene expression and can be examined at the level of exact isoforms. Their magnitude varied substantially across cohorts, and transfer to JHS PBMCs was limited. Genetic follow-up supplied an additional prioritization layer for a subset of transcripts rather than a uniform explanation for the observational findings.

These results extend the gene-level view of adiposity-associated blood transcription. Earlier population studies demonstrated widespread BMI-related expression differences [32]; the present associations ask how a gene's transcript output is distributed after accounting for its overall abundance. Human adipocyte work provides a complementary observation: changes in splicing during differentiation can differ from changes in expression and depend on metabolic phenotype [34]. Taken together, the studies motivate measuring both quantities. The current blood associations do not identify which transcript changes originate in adipose tissue, but they provide a reproducible set for testing relationships between systemic adiposity and RNA processing.

The leading PCBP2 association illustrates both the opportunity and the need for molecular specificity. PCBP2 transcript ENST00000553064.6 was the strongest pooled association for both outcomes, yet its high I2 indicates that a single pooled magnitude compresses substantial cohort variation. Experimental work in mouse embryonic erythropoiesis showed that Pcbp2 regulates Runx1 exon inclusion and erythroid differentiation [35]. This supplies a plausible connection to blood-cell RNA regulation; it does not establish that the measured human PCBP2 ratio changes Pcbp2 activity or explains obesity. The useful next question is whether this exact transcript ratio tracks a cell-intrinsic processing program, variation in blood-cell proportions, or both.

That question can be addressed with measurements aligned to the reported feature. Transcript-specific quantification, junction validation, and long-read confirmation could establish which RNA structures contribute to the PCBP2 signal. Measuring those structures in sorted blood-cell populations would separate shifts within cells from shifts in cellular mixture. Perturbing the relevant transcript while measuring other PCBP2 isoforms, total expression, and downstream splice products would then test whether the association marks a regulatory change. These experiments follow directly from the measured ratio and avoid treating a gene-level annotation as the mechanism of a particular isoform.

Replication and heterogeneity offer complementary information about robustness. Thousands of FHS discoveries retained statistical support and direction in CARDIA or WHI, which makes a purely cohort-specific explanation less likely for that subset. At the same time, moderate whole-universe correlations, high I2 among discoveries, and fewer random-effects hits show that consistency of direction is not equivalent to consistency of magnitude. Age, sex composition, ancestry, blood-cell composition, processing, and upstream model choices are plausible contributors, but the present summaries do not distinguish them. Harmonizing model definitions and testing cohort interactions on verified analysis populations would clarify which associations are broadly stable and which are context dependent.

The PBMC comparison reinforces the importance of that context. Whole-blood and PBMC expression profiles are known to differ [33], making reduced transfer plausible, but JHS also differs from the other cohorts in population and analytical characteristics. Its low replication cannot be attributed to sample compartment alone. Paired whole-blood and PBMC measurements in the same participants would provide a more discriminating comparison. If a transcript ratio replicated within a matched cellular compartment after identical processing and adjustment, it could help distinguish a compartment-associated signature from differences introduced by cohort composition or analysis.

Genetic follow-up narrows a different part of the uncertainty. Inherited regulation of transcript ratios has been demonstrated in human blood [19], allowing selected observational features to be examined using cis instruments. The current screen produced particularly strong estimates for exact RBM5 and WWP2 transcripts, while also highlighting SNRPC and RBM6 candidates. Those results should guide transcript-specific investigation rather than be collapsed into a claim that all isoforms of each gene have the same biological effect. The shared use of cis variants and the compositional relationship between transcripts make that distinction essential.

Most estimable tests and most significant MR findings used a single instrument, leaving little opportunity to compare estimators with different assumptions. Overlap between FHS observational and irQTL samples can also enrich selected effects, and LD pruning does not remove every route from a variant to the outcome outside the tested ratio. Complete regional statistics, independent irQTL replication, and colocalization allowing multiple signals would strengthen interpretation [20,30]. For candidates that pass those checks, measuring the predicted ratio change alongside total gene expression and metabolic readouts in a relevant cell system could determine whether the genetic signal identifies a tractable molecular process. The difference between transformed BMI and ICD-coded obesity also deserves explicit evaluation before comparing MR effect sizes across outcomes.

The functional enrichment results suggest that RNA-related cellular processes are a useful organizing framework for the candidate set. They do not show that every selected transcript changes splicing machinery activity: the analysis maps transcripts to genes and many annotations share genes. A productive follow-up would ask whether validated transcript changes converge on a defined processing event or cellular response. Network analysis could then organize those experimentally anchored relationships. Its planned status in this study is consequential, because no module or hub has yet been demonstrated by the current evidence.

Several limitations set the scope of the conclusions. The analysis used inherited summary estimates, with incomplete documentation of analysis membership, transformations, and cohort-specific models. The provisional Table 1 population therefore cannot supply a verified overall sample size. Isoform ratios are compositional and sensitive to low variation and denominator behavior; the robust coefficient filter is a heuristic, not a measurement-error model. The whole-blood restriction followed earlier result inspection, and blood associations need not generalize to metabolic tissues. Genetic follow-up used European-ancestry inputs, significance-filtered QTL exports, and incomplete instrument coverage. These features limit causal and cross-population interpretation. Longitudinal and mediation questions remain open because the corresponding analyses have not been performed.

The findings provide a basis for moving from broad adiposity-associated transcription toward specific, testable RNA phenotypes. The most informative next step is to combine verified analysis populations and harmonized models with transcript-resolved measurements in defined blood-cell compartments, then prioritize experimentally supported ratios using independent genetic evidence. Such work could establish which reproducible blood RNA changes are useful markers of systemic state and which illuminate a molecular process involved in adiposity.
